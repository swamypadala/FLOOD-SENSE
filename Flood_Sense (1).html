    Automatic alerts so far

      Sample data only. To go live, each input needs a real feed: rain and gauge readings from your river agency, elevation from a terrain survey, population from census or ward records, roads and shelters from your disaster authority, and alerts from the official warning service.

      Read the signs early

      River floods build over hours or days. Flash floods can arrive in minutes. The signs below tell you which one you are facing, and they often show up before any official alert reaches your phone.

      In the sky and upstream

      • Heavy rain that does not let up for hours, especially in hills or catchments upstream of you.
      • News of a cloudburst, a dam or barrage release, or a breach somewhere above you.
      • Rain stopping where you are while rivers keep rising. Water takes time to travel down.

      At the river

      • The level rising steadily, or faster than usual, past marks you know.
      • Water turning thick and muddy, or carrying branches and rubbish.
      • A louder roar from the river.
      • A stream that suddenly drops in heavy rain. A blockage upstream may be holding water back.

      Around your home

      • Drains backing up or pooling on low streets.
      • Water seeping up through floors or from the ground.
      • Low roads and underpasses going under.
      • Neighbours moving vehicles and belongings to higher ground.

      Some floods give minutes, not hours

      In steep valleys, near small streams, or after a cloudburst, water can arrive almost without warning. If you see these signs there, move to high ground first and check alerts afterwards.

      Watch or warning?

      WatchConditions could lead to flooding. This is your time to prepare: pack, plan the route, check on neighbours.
      WarningFlooding is happening or about to. Act on your plan now and leave if you are in a low-lying area.

      Names and colours differ by country. In India, IMD weather warnings are colour-coded: orange means be prepared, red means take action.

      Where reliable alerts come from

      • Your national weather service, such as IMD in India.
      • The river agency that publishes gauge levels and forecasts, such as the Central Water Commission.
      • Your state or district disaster management authority and control room.
      • Official phone alerts and SMS, local radio, and loudspeaker announcements.
      • Neighbours and village or ward volunteers. Pass on what you hear, but check it against an official source.

      How the water is predicted

      Forecasters follow rain from the sky into the river. Rain gauges, radar and satellites show how much has fallen. Gauges along the river show how fast the water is rising. Because floodwater takes time to travel, a rise upstream is an early warning for everyone downstream.

      Never test the water

      Most flood deaths happen when people walk or drive into water that looks shallow. Moving water is far stronger than it looks.

      15 cmMoving water this deep can knock an adult off their feet.
      30 cmEnough to float many cars and lose control of them.
      60 cmCan carry away most vehicles, including large ones.

      Also stay away from fallen power lines, open drains and manholes hidden under water, and flooded basements.

        Guide others out

        Evacuations work when everyone knows one plan. If you are a ward member, volunteer, teacher or neighbourhood leader, these steps help people leave on time.

        • Make a list ahead of time of households that need help: older people, people with disabilities, pregnant women, young children, bedridden patients.
        • Agree one route and one shelter per area. Check that the route stays above water and mark it clearly.
        • Set the trigger in advance, such as a gauge reading or an official warning, so nobody waits for permission.
        • Alert by every channel: phone calls, messaging groups, loudspeakers and door-to-door. Use plain words: "Leave now. Go to the school on the hill."
        • Move people who need help first, and send households out in groups, not alone.
        • Count heads at the shelter and keep the list current, so rescuers know who is still missing.

        Emergency in India: call 112

        If you are outside India, use your local emergency number and write it into your plan above.
